Making public money legible

I teach undergraduate public budgeting and finance at the O’Neill School. My aim is straightforward: students should leave able to read a real budget, follow a dollar from revenue to service, and argue about fiscal trade-offs with evidence.

Introduction to Public Budgeting and Finance

A first course in government finance for undergraduates across public affairs, policy, and management. The course covers the budget cycle and its politics; revenue systems and tax incidence; expenditure analysis; debt, capital budgeting, and financial condition; and the intergovernmental transfers that tie federal, state, and local budgets together. Students work directly with published budget and financial-report data.

Approach

Teaching philosophy

Public budgeting is often taught as accounting. I teach it as decision-making under constraint — because that is what practitioners actually face.

Every abstract concept in the course is anchored to a document a student could plausibly encounter in their first public-sector job: a city’s adopted budget, a state Medicaid appropriation, an annual comprehensive financial report. Once students can find the number, the theory has somewhere to land.

I also try to keep the stakes visible. Budgets are the clearest statement a government makes about its priorities, and students engage far more deeply when a line item is connected to the service, the community, and the trade-off behind it.
